\begin{abstract}
Machine learning research moves in waves. At ICLR, the share of submissions building on a foundation model rose from
0.3\% in 2017 to 54\% in 2026, while the share proposing a new architecture fell from 24\% to 8\%, and keyword waves
such as graph neural networks and federated learning rose and fell within about five years. We ask how a field that
changes this fast balances tradition and innovation, at three levels: how the field changes, who brings new work, and
what peer review rewards. We study 43,480 ICLR submissions from 2017--2026, including rejected and withdrawn papers,
with registered tests run once and replicated on 11,099 held-out papers. Topic maps fitted with hindsight manufacture
waves, but displacement is real: where foundation models spread, new architectures fade. Contrary to a herding
account, papers by newcomers to a topic more often propose new methods. Review shows no detectable reward for
following the crowd; acceptance instead goes with arriving early in a wave and with teams anchored in their own
expertise. Innovation comes from outsiders; acceptance favours insiders and early arrivals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Machine learning (ML) research changes fast, and it changes in waves. At ICLR, the share of submissions that build on
a foundation model rose from 0.3\% in 2017 to 54\% in 2026, while the share proposing a new architecture fell from
24\% to 8\%. Author keywords show the same pattern at a finer grain (Figure~\ref{fig:waves}). Generative adversarial
networks peaked at 8.1\% of submissions in 2018 and have almost disappeared. Graph neural networks, federated learning
and self-supervised learning each rose and fell within about five years. Large language models went from nothing to
16.7\% of submissions in four years. Even titles follow waves: titles of the form ``NAME: subtitle'' rose from 9\% to
41\% of submissions.

Such waves raise an old question about science. \citet{kuhn1977} called it the \emph{essential tension}: science needs
tradition, the shared methods that let work accumulate, and innovation, the departures that open new directions. A
fast-moving field makes this tension sharp, in three ways. A wave can displace earlier lines of work
\citep{kuhn1962,bommasani2021}. It can be carried by imitators who follow what is popular
\citep{abrahamson1991,bikhchandani1992}, or by outsiders who bring new ideas \citep{jeppesen2010,azoulay2019}. And
peer review may reward conformity, because novel work is risky and often undervalued
\citep{foster2015,wang2017,boudreau2016}.

We therefore ask how ML research balances tradition and innovation during the foundation-model shift, at three
levels:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Field:} How does the field change, and can its waves be measured without hindsight?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{People:} Who brings new work, and who follows?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Review:} Does peer review reward tradition, innovation or following?
\end{itemize}

We study 43,480 ICLR submissions from 2017 to 2026, built from our own snapshots of official OpenReview records. They
include rejected and withdrawn papers, so what we observe is not only what survived review. We place papers in
content-based topics using SPECTER2 embeddings \citep{singh2023}, label each paper's contribution type with a fixed
LLM codebook, and identify newcomers to a topic from author identifiers. The core hypotheses were registered before
any test was run \citep{nosek2018}, run once with frozen code, and then tested again on 11,099 held-out papers that we
had never analysed. Because we report about twenty findings, each carries an evidence tier, from ``registered and
replicated'' to ``descriptive'' (Table~\ref{tab:findings}). All results are associations; none is a causal effect of
choosing a topic, a team or a type of contribution.

\paragraph{Field.} The waves are real, but the standard way of measuring them is not neutral. A topic map fitted on
all years at once, as is common in studies of research trends \citep{hall2008}, is dominated by recent papers: it
finds 29 emerging topics where a map fitted on 2017--2020 papers finds 8. On maps without hindsight, displacement still
holds: where foundation models spread, new architectures fade, in discovery and on held-out papers.

\paragraph{People.} We registered the herding prediction, and it failed. Papers by newcomers to a topic are more
often method papers and less often benchmark papers, in discovery and again on held-out papers. First-time teams are,
if anything, slower than other teams to adopt trending keywords and foundation models.

\paragraph{Review.} We find no detectable reward for following the crowd: the test for crowded topics is
inconclusive, and trendy titles carry no premium. Timing and anchoring matter instead. Papers listing young, rising
keywords are accepted slightly more often, and a keyword's acceptance gap falls after its share of submissions peaks;
federated learning is the starkest case in discovery. Papers far from their team's own earlier work are accepted less
often, and papers whose team includes a topic insider more often.

Together, these findings point to one pattern: \emph{innovation comes from outsiders, while acceptance favours
insiders and early arrivals}. Waves go with rewards for those who ride them early, not for those who follow. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} We show that topic maps fitted with hindsight manufacture research waves and can
    reverse trend conclusions, and we give a cheap check: refit the map on early years only.
  \item \textbf{Evidence.} We give a three-level account of how a fast-moving field changes, who drives the change and
    what review rewards, with every finding labelled by its evidence tier and the core findings replicated on
    held-out papers.
  \item \textbf{Design.} We show that a registered, outcome-blind, single-run protocol with a held-out replication
    is feasible for observational studies of science.
\end{itemize}
