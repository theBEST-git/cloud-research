### Bottom line

1. **Names.** Call the two routes **imitation** and **continuity**. Define them by their *reference point*:
   - Imitation is conformity that refers to **what others are doing now**.
   - Continuity is conformity that refers to **an accumulated record**: the team's own past work, and the topic's past
     work as carried by a member of the team.

   Use "anchored" only as a defined adjective (Section 3.1). Never use it as the technical term.
2. **Theory gives both routes old and well-separated roots, but they are rarely tested against each other.**
   - Imitation:
     - frequency imitation (Haunschild and Miner);
     - mimetic isomorphism (DiMaggio and Powell);
     - information- and rivalry-based imitation (Lieberman and Asaba);
     - bandwagons (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf);
     - reputational herding (Scharfstein and Stein);
     - the cascade models already in the lit files.
   - Continuity:
     - exploitation and competency traps (March; Levitt and March);
     - local search (Stuart and Podolny; Ahuja and Lampert);
     - absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal);
     - lock-in (Arthur);
     - Bourdieu's "succession strategies";
     - in science of science, the pivot penalty, the chaperone effect, switching costs (Myers) and the conservative
       strategy distribution (Foster et al.).
3. **The division-of-cognitive-labor models separate the two routes inside one model.** Weisberg and Muldoon compare
   three kinds of agent:
   - agents who search from their own position and ignore others;
   - "followers", who are drawn to where others have been;
   - "mavericks", who avoid where others have been.

   This is the cleanest formal precedent for our distinction. Weisberg's later "herding" paper and Baddeley's essays
   instead blend risk-avoidance, path dependence and social influence under one word. We can point to that blending as
   the conceptual gap.
4. **Closest explicit precedent.** The closest framing of our distinction is the 2025 QSS review of research topic choice
   by Xiang, Romero and Teplitskiy. It names "social context ... and path dependence" as rival drivers of topic choice,
   and it lists the costs of entry and switching as a research gap. I found **no** study that tests the two routes against
   each other at entry and at the gate. The search was limited, so say "to our knowledge".
5. **The frame is most discriminating at entry and at the gate, and weakest at the field level.** At the field level,
   both routes, and also efficient choice, predict convergence on a package (Section 3.3).

### 1.1 Continuity: exploitation, local search, path dependence (organisations and economics)

#### March 1991: exploration and exploitation (`march1991exploration`)

- **Citation.** March, James G. 1991. Exploration and exploitation in organizational learning. *Organization Science*
  2(1): 71-87. DOI 10.1287/orsc.2.1.71.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR; pages also on RePEc. CONTENT-ABSTRACT.
- **Claims (abstract):**
  - It studies the relation between exploring new possibilities and exploiting old certainties.
  - Adaptive processes refine exploitation faster than exploration. This makes them effective in the short run and
    self-destructive in the long run.
  - It has two models: mutual learning between members and an organisational code, and competition for primacy.
- **Secondary only, check before citing.** In the mutual-learning model, slow learners and some turnover of members keep
  the diversity the code needs, and in a changing world they keep it from locking in.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Continuity is March's *exploitation*: refining one's existing competence. It pays quickly and predictably.
  - Imitation is not March's exploration. It is a third thing: borrowing others' current choices.
  - March thus predicts that a short-cycle gate rewards continuity. A conference decision is the archetype of a
    proximate, short-run payoff.
  - The turnover result, once checked, would frame topic entrants as the organisation's source of exploration. That
    fits H2b′.
- **Use.** Introduction and discussion: the canonical name for the continuity route's logic. **Do not** call
  continuity "exploitation" in the text, because "exploiting foundation models" would be ambiguous.

#### Levitt and March 1988: organizational learning (`levitt1988organizational`)

- **Citation.** Levitt, Barbara and James G. March. 1988. Organizational learning. *Annual Review of Sociology* 14:
  319-338. DOI 10.1146/annurev.so.14.080188.001535.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR. Crossref gives pages 319-338. One scan seen in search gives 319-340; use Crossref.
  - CONTENT-SECONDARY for "competency trap": the abstract was not retrieved; the attribution of the term comes from
    secondary sources.
- **Claims.**
  - Organisational learning is routine-based, history-dependent and target-oriented.
  - It covers learning from direct experience, learning from the experience of others, and organisational memory.
  - Competency trap (secondary): good results with a procedure lead to more experience with it, even when another
    procedure would be better.
- **How it frames our routes.** It names both routes in one review:
  - "learning from direct experience" is continuity;
  - "learning from the experience of others" is imitation.

  This is probably the oldest single citation that separates them. The competency trap gives the long-run cost of
  continuity, which is the counterpart to our gate's short-run premium.
- **Use.** The definitions paragraph.

#### Cohen and Levinthal 1990: absorptive capacity (`cohen1990absorptive`)

- **Citation.** Cohen, Wesley M. and Daniel A. Levinthal. 1990. Absorptive capacity: A new perspective on learning and
  innovation. *Administrative Science Quarterly* 35(1): 128-152. DOI 10.2307/2393553.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for volume, issue, start page and DOI. The end page 152 is from common citation, not seen in a record.
  - CONTENT-ABSTRACT (Scinapse and Duke pages).
- **Claim.** The ability to recognise, assimilate and use outside knowledge depends largely on prior related knowledge.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - This is the mechanism of continuity at the producer side: you can absorb a new package only through your existing
    record.
  - It predicts that **teams with no record adopt a field's new package more slowly**. That is the pattern in the
    first-time-team FM gap (DP:360-362).
  - Imitation theory predicts the opposite, because the agents with the least private information herd most
    (Bikhchandani et al.; FW:57).
- **Use.** Entry predictions (Section 3.3).

#### Stuart and Podolny 1996: local search (`stuart1996local`)

- **Citation.** Stuart, Toby E. and Joel M. Podolny. 1996. Local search and the evolution of technological capabilities.
  *Strategic Management Journal* 17(S1): 21-38. DOI 10.1002/smj.4250171004.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for the DOI, volume and pages. Crossref's issued date (2007) is the online-archive date; the authors' CV gives
    1996, 17:21-38.
  - CONTENT-ABSTRACT (Columbia page).
- **Claims.**
  - Evolutionary theories assume that "local search" constrains the direction of R&D.
  - The paper measures how locally bounded firms' search is, in the semiconductor industry from 1982 to 1992.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Our far-jump measure is a measure of how local a team's search is. It is the cosine between a paper and the closest
    experienced co-author's earlier ICLR work (DP:231-232).
  - Continuity is local search; imitation is search anchored on the crowd.
- **Use.** The definitions paragraph; the measure's justification.

#### Ahuja and Lampert 2001: familiarity, maturity and propinquity traps (`ahuja2001entrepreneurship`)

- **Citation.** Ahuja, Gautam and Curba Morris Lampert. 2001. Entrepreneurship in the large corporation: A longitudinal
  study of how established firms create breakthrough inventions. *Strategic Management Journal* 22(6-7): 521-543.
  DOI 10.1002/smj.176.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR. CONTENT-ABSTRACT.
- **Claims (abstract).**
  - Three traps limit breakthroughs:
    - familiarity: favouring the familiar;
    - maturity: favouring the mature;
    - propinquity: searching near existing solutions.
  - Experimenting with novel, emerging and pioneering technologies helps escape them.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - The *propinquity* trap is continuity with one's own record.
  - The *maturity* trap is continuity with the field's established record.
  - Neither is imitation. This is a vocabulary for saying that continuity has a long-run cost (cf. Rzhetsky et al.;
    FW:155) even when the gate rewards it now.
- **Use.** Discussion, one sentence.

#### Arthur 1989: lock-in (`arthur1989competing`)

- **Citation.** Arthur, W. Brian. 1989. Competing technologies, increasing returns, and lock-in by historical events.
  *The Economic Journal* 99(394): 116-131. DOI 10.2307/2234208.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for the start page. RePEc gives 116-131; one source gives 106-131. Use 116-131.
  - CONTENT-ABSTRACT (RePEc).
- **Claims.**
  - Under increasing returns to adoption, chance early events can lock an economy into a path that need not be
    efficient.
  - Expectations about others' choices can make lock-in more likely.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - This is the field-level form of path dependence. A package that improves as more people build on it (foundation
    models) spreads without anyone imitating for imitation's sake.
  - It gives a non-fashion reading of H1d that is distinct from both cascades and Sutton's efficiency argument.
  - Note that Arthur's mechanism *includes* expectations about others. At the field level, the two routes blur. This is
    why the frame is weakest there.
- **Use.** Field-level predictions; discussion of H1d.

### 1.2 Imitation: frequency, mimetic and reputational routes (organisations and economics)

#### DiMaggio and Powell 1983: mimetic isomorphism (`dimaggio1983iron`)

- **Citation.** DiMaggio, Paul J. and Walter W. Powell. 1983. The iron cage revisited: Institutional isomorphism and
  collective rationality in organizational fields. *American Sociological Review* 48(2): 147-160. DOI 10.2307/2095101.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for the start page; the end page 160 is confirmed by a search result.
  - CONTENT-SECONDARY for the mimetic mechanism. The three processes (coercive, mimetic, normative) are seen in several
    sources; the mimetic section itself was not read.
- **Claim.** Under uncertainty and ambiguous goals, organisations model themselves on others (mimetic isomorphism).
- **How it frames our routes.** This is the textbook imitation route, and it predicts imitation where uncertainty is
  highest:
  - for **entrants to a topic**, who lack a record there;
  - in **young, fast-moving topics**.

  Our entrants do the opposite: they bring methods and are slower to take up trends (H2b′/H2c′; DP:315, 360). This is
  the cleanest theoretical foil for the entry results.
- **Use.** Introduction: the imitation prediction for entrants.

#### Haunschild and Miner 1997: modes of imitation (`haunschild1997modes`)

- **Citation.** Haunschild, Pamela R. and Anne S. Miner. 1997. Modes of interorganizational imitation: The effects of
  outcome salience and uncertainty. *Administrative Science Quarterly* 42(3): 472-500. DOI 10.2307/2393735.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for the start page; 472-500 is confirmed by a search result.
  - CONTENT-ABSTRACT.
- **Claims (abstract).** There are three modes of selective imitation:
  - *frequency* (doing what many others do);
  - *trait* (copying certain kinds of others);
  - *outcome* (copying what seemed to work).

  The three operate independently. Uncertainty strengthens frequency imitation and some trait imitation, but not outcome
  imitation. The test uses 539 acquisitions from 1988 to 1993.
- **How it frames our routes.** It splits the imitation route into measurable parts, and each maps onto one of our
  measures:
  - **Frequency imitation** is what our "crowding", "hot topic" and "trending keyword" measures index.
  - **Outcome imitation** would be following topics with high past acceptance. The premia regression to the mean (b 0.60
    held-out; DC:376) says such signals are about half noise at the topic-year level (DP:325), so outcome imitation
    of topic acceptance would chase noise.
  - **Trait imitation** (copying prestigious labs) is not measured by us.
- **Use.** Definitions. Say which imitation mode each test addresses.

#### Lieberman and Asaba 2006: why firms imitate (`lieberman2006why`)

- **Citation.** Lieberman, Marvin B. and Shigeru Asaba. 2006. Why do firms imitate each other? *Academy of Management
  Review* 31(2): 366-385. DOI 10.5465/amr.2006.20208686.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR. CONTENT-ABSTRACT.
- **Claims (abstract).** Theories of imitation fall into two groups:
  - information-based: following those thought to know more;
  - rivalry-based: imitating to keep competitive parity or to limit rivalry.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Our imitation route covers both motives:
    - cascades and Scharfstein–Stein reputational herding are information-based;
    - races to hot topics (Hill and Stein 2025; NR:322) are rivalry-based.
  - For a field, rivalry-based imitation predicts crowding *into* hot topics. Borjas and Doran (below) show the opposite
    response, moving *away* from crowded areas.
- **Use.** A one-line typology in the background.

#### Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1993: institutional and competitive bandwagons (`abrahamson1993institutional`)

- **Citation.** Abrahamson, Eric and Lori Rosenkopf. 1993. Institutional and competitive bandwagons: Using mathematical
  modeling as a tool to explore innovation diffusion. *Academy of Management Review* 18(3): 487-517. DOI
  10.2307/258906.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR for the start page; 487-517 per the co-author's CV.
  - CONTENT-ABSTRACT (Columbia page).
- **Claims.**
  - Bandwagon pressure can be institutional (non-adopters stand out) or competitive (fear of falling behind adopters).
  - Innovations with ambiguous returns can spread as bandwagons, even to adopters who expect negative returns.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - The imitation route's key prediction is that **adoption can spread without a reward for adopting**.
  - Our gate evidence fits this:
    - there is no detectable premium for crowding (H3a, C);
    - there is no premium for branded titles (ratio 1.02, D\*).
  - A bandwagon can therefore exist without a reward. "No premium" does not rule out imitation among producers; it only
    rules out the *rewarded-imitation* reading at the gate. This is an important honesty point for the paper.
- **Use.** The gate section: how to read the absence of a premium.

#### Scharfstein and Stein 1990: reputational herding (`scharfstein1990herd`)

- **Citation.** Scharfstein, David S. and Jeremy C. Stein. 1990. Herd behavior and investment. *American Economic
  Review* 80(3): 465-479.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-WEB: RePEc and the author's Harvard page. There is no DOI (JSTOR era). Crossref has only the 2000 Reply, AER
    90(3): 705-706.
  - CONTENT-SECONDARY. Managers concerned with their reputation imitate others and ignore private information. The
    "sharing-the-blame" phrase often attached to the paper was **not** confirmed; do not use it.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - This is the career-concern version of imitation. Agents imitate because evaluators judge them relative to others.
  - It predicts that **if** evaluators reward conformity to peers, imitation is individually rational. That is exactly
    what the gate tests check.
- **Use.** The motivation for testing the gate.

### 1.3 Division of cognitive labor and the reward system (philosophy and sociology of science)

#### Kitcher 1990: the division of cognitive labor (`kitcher1990division`)

- **Citation.** Kitcher, Philip. 1990. The division of cognitive labor. *The Journal of Philosophy* 87(1): 5-22. DOI
  10.2307/2026796.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR (start page) and BIB-WEB (5-22). CONTENT-SECONDARY.
- **Claim.** A community of credit-seeking scientists can spread its effort across approaches better than a community of
  pure truth-seekers. In the latter, everyone pursues the currently leading approach, which is collectively irrational.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - The "everyone works on the leading approach" outcome is the imitation route at the community level.
  - Kitcher's point is that the *reward system* can counteract it. The question becomes empirical: does the reward ICLR
    gives (acceptance) counteract the pull of the crowd, or amplify it?
  - Our gate shows no detectable premium for the crowd, and a premium for continuity.
- **Use.** Introduction: why the gate matters theoretically.

#### Strevens 2003: the priority rule (`strevens2003role`)

- **Citation.** Strevens, Michael. 2003. The role of the priority rule in science. *The Journal of Philosophy* 100(2):
  55-79. DOI 10.5840/jphil2003100224.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR. CONTENT-SECONDARY (Romero 2017 and Heesen, as seen in search).
- **Claim.** Rewarding only the first discoverer (Merton's priority rule) gives incentives for an efficient division of
  cognitive labour.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Priority is the theoretical reason why *late* imitators should not be rewarded, and why arriving early can be.
  - It turns "timing" into the discriminating test between the routes. Rewarded imitation predicts that late riders do
    as well as early ones. A priority-based reward system predicts that the premium falls as a wave matures.
  - Our timing results:
    - A1, held-out +3.25 [−0.18, +6.69], B weak (DC:375);
    - PH6, held-out −5.2 [−11.1, +0.7], B weak, leaning on six inspected keywords (DC:378; DP:391-392).

    Both lean towards priority, weakly.
- **Use.** The gate section, with Merton 1957 (`merton1957priorities`, NR:63).

#### Weisberg and Muldoon 2009: epistemic landscapes (`weisberg2009epistemic`)

- **Citation.** Weisberg, Michael and Ryan Muldoon. 2009. Epistemic landscapes and the division of cognitive labor.
  *Philosophy of Science* 76(2): 225-252. DOI 10.1086/644786.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR. CONTENT-ABSTRACT (as reproduced in search results).
  - The label "controls" for the independent agents is from memory and was **not** confirmed. Say "agents who ignore
    others' findings".
- **Claims (abstract).**
  - An agent-based model of scientists searching an epistemic landscape, under three strategies:
    - work independently of what others have found;
    - "followers", biased towards approaches others have already explored;
    - "mavericks", who avoid approaches already taken.
  - Pure follower populations do worse than independent agents. Mavericks do best.
  - Mixed maverick-follower populations are ideal in many domains.
- **How it frames our routes.** This is **the** formal precedent:
  - independent agents climb from their own position, which is continuity;
  - followers are imitation;
  - mavericks are anti-imitation.

  The model makes the distinction we draw. It also shows that the two routes have different collective consequences.
- **Caveat: Alexander, Himmelreich and Thompson 2015** (`alexander2015epistemic`).
  - Citation: *Philosophy of Science* 82(3): 424-453, DOI 10.1086/681766. BIB-CR. CONTENT-PRIMARY: abstract read on
    Crossref.
  - They argue that seeking novelty need not be optimal, that the case for dividing cognitive labour has not been shown,
    and that one should be sceptical of social learning on complex landscapes.
  - Cite both. We use the models for their *taxonomy* of search strategies, not for their welfare conclusions.
- **Use.** The definitions paragraph. This is the strongest single anchor for "two routes".

#### Weisberg 2013: modeling herding (`weisberg2013modeling`)

- **Verification.** BIB-LIT (FW:73).
- **Claim.** Researchers herd because they avoid risk; herding is robust; incentives for risky research help a little.
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Weisberg's "herding" is *risk-avoiding clustering*. Our data say this kind of conformity takes the continuity form
    (staying near one's own record) rather than the imitation form.
  - Recommended wording: "what Weisberg (2013) models as herding, risk-avoiding clustering, appears here as continuity
    with one's own record, not as imitation of the crowd". This sharpens landscape.md's point (LS:91-92).

#### Thoma 2015: the epistemic division of labor revisited (`thoma2015epistemic`)

- **Citation.** Thoma, Johanna. 2015. The epistemic division of labor revisited. *Philosophy of Science* 82(3): 454-472.
  DOI 10.1086/681768.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR. CONTENT-SECONDARY for the details; only the opening of the abstract was seen.
  - The preprint abstract (seen in search) says that division of labour helps only when scientists are not too

#### Zollman 2010: transient diversity (`zollman2010epistemic`)

- **Citation.** Zollman, Kevin J. S. 2010. The epistemic benefit of transient diversity. *Erkenntnis* 72(1): 17-35. DOI
  10.1007/s10670-009-9194-6.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR. The issue is dated 2010; Crossref's date is the 2009 online-first date.
  - CONTENT-SECONDARY: transient, not permanent, diversity is the epistemic goal; communities that share information too

- **Framing.** It is the network form of the imitation route's collective cost: premature convergence through

#### Bourdieu 1975: the scientific field (`bourdieu1975specificity`)

- **Citation.** Bourdieu, Pierre. 1975. The specificity of the scientific field and the social conditions of the
  progress of reason. *Social Science Information* 14(6): 19-47. DOI 10.1177/053901847501400602.
- **Verification.**
  - BIB-CR.
  - CONTENT-PRIMARY. I read the English text in a PDF of the article, saved in my scratchpad as
    `dl_bourdieu/b.txt`, lines 568-640 and 747-755.
- **Claims (from the text):**
  - The scientific field is a competitive struggle for the monopoly of scientific authority, which is a form of capital.
    Possessing some scientific capital favours acquiring more.
  - "The dominant are committed to conservation strategies."
  - "New entrants" may be oriented either:
    - towards "the risk-free investments of succession strategies", which bring "limited innovations within authorised
      limits"; or
    - towards "subversion strategies, infinitely more costly and more hazardous".
  - Journals "consecrate productions faithful to the principles of official science".
  - In highly autonomous fields, "the opposition between succession strategies and subversion strategies increasingly
    tends to lose its meaning".
- **How it frames our routes.**
  - Bourdieu's newcomers do not imitate the crowd. They choose between **succession** (continuity with the field's
    authorised record) and **subversion**.
  - Gatekeepers consecrate continuity with "official science".
  - This is a sociological anchor for both halves of our story:
    - entrants are not imitators;
    - the gate rewards continuity.
  - It also adds a twist our data fit. Within a single highly competitive field, succession-type continuity (a team's own
    record, a topic insider) is rewarded at the gate. Meanwhile entrants bring method proposals, which is neither pure
    succession nor subversion.
- **Wording cautions.**
  - Do not equate method papers with "subversion": a method paper is a contribution *type* (DC:313-314).
  - Foster et al. 2015 already frame tradition vs innovation in Bourdieu's field theory (OUT:102). Cite Bourdieu
    directly for the newcomer passage.
- **Use.** Introduction or background. This is the strongest sociology-of-science anchor for continuity at the gate.

### 1.4 Empirical precedents in science of science

#### Xiang, Romero and Teplitskiy 2025: research topic choice (`xiang2025research`)

- **Citation.** Xiang, Sidney, Daniel Romero and Misha Teplitskiy. 2025. Research topic choice: Motivations, strategies,
  and consequences. *Quantitative Science Studies* 6: 623-651. DOI 10.1162/qss_a_00365.
- **Verification.** BIB-CR. CONTENT-PRIMARY: the abstract was read on Crossref. The body is described only through a
  search summary; it was not read.
- **Claims (abstract).**
  - A review of theory and evidence on topic choice.
  - Its themes include whether choices are "broadly reward maximizing or driven by social context, identities, and path
    dependence".
  - It names as gaps:
    - "the types and impacts of costs impeding topic entry and switching";
    - "the causal mechanisms associating topics with rewards".
- **How it frames our routes.** This is the most recent explicit statement that social context and path dependence are
  distinct drivers of topic choice, and that entry costs and topic rewards are open. Our design addresses both gaps:
  - entry: H2b′/H2c′ and the first-time-team profiles;
  - rewards: the gate tests.

  This is the best single citation for the gap sentence.
- **Use.** Gap statement. Read the body before claiming what it says about specific models.

## 3. One-page theoretical frame (proposal)

### 3.1 Names and definitions

We distinguish two routes by which a fast-moving field can become more uniform. Each produces sameness, but each
refers to a different benchmark when deciding what to work on, and when a gatekeeper decides what to accept.

- **Imitation** is conformity to *what others are doing now*: choosing topics, packages or framings because they are
  popular or rising. Examples are frequency imitation (Haunschild and Miner 1997) and cascades (Bikhchandani et al.
  1992).
- **Continuity** is conformity to *an accumulated record*: choosing work close to what one's team has already done
  (local search; March 1991; Stuart and Podolny 1996), or entering a topic with someone who already carries its
  record. The second form follows the chaperone effect (Sekara et al. 2018) and Bourdieu's "succession strategies"
  (1975).

Weisberg and Muldoon's (2009) model contains exactly this split: "followers", against agents who search from their
own position. We call work that conforms by continuity **anchored**. Define this at first use, with a footnote: *not*
the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman 1974).

**Operational definitions** (do not change them in the text):
- **Imitation** is measured as:
  - topic crowding (maturity; DC:153);
  - hot-topic status (DC:153);
  - keyword state, i.e. Emerging, Mainstream or Cooling (DP:305-316);
  - FM adoption (DP:356-362);
  - branded titles (DP:106-133);
  - same-year twins (DP:78-79).
- **Continuity** is measured as:
  - F_max, the cosine to the closest experienced co-author's earlier ICLR work, since 2021 (DP:231-232);
  - the within-author slope (DP:241-243);
  - the topic insider: an author with at least one earlier ICLR submission, any outcome, in the paper's k100 topic
    (PRE:1569). It is estimated at fixed team size and number of experienced authors, so it measures "topic familiarity
    net of general ICLR experience" (PRE:1659; DP:285).

  This settles landscape.md open check 1 (LS:490-492): an insider need **not** have an earlier *accepted* paper. The
  proven-record question is H4 (+4.6, Holm 0.088; DP:291), and the per-head decomposition (DP:294-296) is D.

### 3.3 What the frame claims, and what it does not

**The claim.** In the one venue we test, the herding account fails where it is sharpest:
- **At entry:** entrants bring proposals rather than follow-on measurement.
- **At the gate:** there is no detectable premium for following.

The conformity we *can* detect at the gate is continuity.

The field-level convergence (H1d) is real. But the field level cannot tell the routes apart: lock-in, package
bandwagons and efficient choice all predict it. The paper should say so rather than claim H1d for continuity.

**What we cannot test (state it in the text):**
1. Whether entrants flock to hot topics (H2a, C).
2. Whether the continuity premium reflects better-informed, better-made papers or evaluator conservatism.
   - These are Li 2017's expertise vs bias (NR:233) and Hill et al.'s two mechanisms (reputation; lower quality while
     catching up).
   - The double-blind setting rules out neither: arXiv can de-anonymise authors (CP:34).
3. Whether producers imitate *without* reward (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf). Absence of a gate premium does not show
   absence of a bandwagon.
4. Generalisation beyond ICLR (DC:56).

**Wording guardrails specific to this frame:**
- "Continuity", "anchored" and "imitation" label measures. They are not motives: we observe positions, not choices.
- Never write "teams choose to stay close" (DC:42, 48).
- "Method paper" is a contribution type, not "subversion" or novelty (DC:313-314).
- The insider premium within its module: Holm 0.019 holds within the module family only; across modules it would be
  about 0.17 (DP:54-55).
- Held-out results are "a pre-registered out-of-sample replication, not a certified untouched holdout" (DC:43-44).

### 3.5 Suggested definitions paragraph (draft; every claim traceable above)

> Fields can become uniform by two routes. Under *imitation*, researchers take their cue from what others are doing
> now (Bikhchandani et al. 1992; DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Haunschild and Miner 1997). Under *continuity*, they take
> it from an accumulated record: their own past work (March 1991; Stuart and Podolny 1996), or the record of a topic
> carried by a collaborator who already works in it (Sekara et al. 2018). Weisberg and Muldoon (2009) model the
> difference as "followers" versus scientists who search from their own position. Writing on herding in science often
> merges the two (Weisberg 2013). We call work that conforms by continuity *anchored*. This is a position relative to
> a record, not the anchoring heuristic of Tversky and Kahneman (1974). The two routes make different predictions for
> who enters a topic and with what, and for what peer review rewards.
